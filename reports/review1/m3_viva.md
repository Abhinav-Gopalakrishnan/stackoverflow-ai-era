# Member 3: Viva Questions for Problem Framing & EDA (M3-VIVA)

Nine likely examiner viva questions, with concise model answers grounded in `docs/problem_statement.md`, `docs/eda_plan.md` and the executed sections `[M3-05]` to `[M3-12]` of `notebooks/03_eda.ipynb`. Every AI-related statement is associational; nothing here claims causation.

| **1** | **Why is an accepted answer within 7 days the target, and how is it different from your secondary target?** | `target_resolved_7d` is whether a question has an **accepted** answer within 168 hours of posting; `target_answered_7d` is whether it has **any** answer within the same window. The gap is large and real: **76.0%** answered versus **42.7%** accepted. So acceptance is only a proxy for resolution — it also depends on the asker returning and marking an answer. I report both targets side by side everywhere, and I track `accepted_given_answered` (**57.0% pre-Nov 2022 → 50.1% after**) as its own KPI, because that ratio separates "nobody answered" from "the asker never accepted". A question nobody answered and a question answered but not accepted are different business problems with different fixes. |
| **2** | **Your charts all cut the timeline at November 2022. Does that prove AI assistants caused the decline in question volume?** | No. It is a **cutoff, not a cause**. The date comes from our own dated event list (`docs/event_list.csv`), and I draw it as a labelled vertical annotation on every time series so readers can see where the break sits relative to Copilot GA, the ChatGPT launch, the Stack Overflow ChatGPT ban and the 2023 moderator strike. Two confounds are documented rather than hidden: our own background source (Pragmatic Engineer, in `docs/background_sources.md`) shows decline beginning well before 2022, and the ACM study (Burtch, Lee and Chen, 2024) is itself correlational, attributing about a 12% fall in daily visits to ChatGPT. The moderator strike and the AI-content policy are simultaneous governance shocks. Every finding in the deck and in `initial_findings.md` is worded *associated with* or *consistent with*, never *caused by*. |
| **3** | **How does sampling weighting enter your EDA, and why can you not just count questions per month?** | Before 2025 Member 1 sampled one weekday plus one weekend day per tag-month (seed 42), an empirical **6.5-7.5%** of days; from January 2025 collection became a **100% census** because site volume had collapsed far enough to make it feasible. So raw monthly counts jump at the boundary for a reason that has nothing to do with user behaviour — the sampling rate changed. Every monthly trend I publish uses the inverse-probability weights in `monthly_tag_tables.csv`, which reproduces population volume within about 1% annually. Separately, months with fewer than 30 sampled questions are **greyed out and flagged** using Member 2's `low_sample` column rather than deleted, so thin periods stay visible instead of being silently smoothed. |
| **4** | **How was the EDA planned, and how do you know it answers the research questions?** | `docs/eda_plan.md` is the contract, agreed with the team **before** any chart was drawn. It maps eight chart IDs (EDA-01 to EDA-08) to a target research question, the required data asset and owner, the visualisation type, and the task owner. EDA-01 to EDA-07 are mine; EDA-08 is Member 1's API-coverage analysis. It also fixes two presentation rules I follow throughout: every chart title states the analytical takeaway rather than describing the axes, and milestone dates are overlaid consistently. So each section of `03_eda.ipynb` traces to a numbered plan row and a research question — RQ3 for the volume and latency work, RQ1 for the structural-feature work, RQ2 for the dual-target comparison. |
| **5** | **You say every feature is statistically significant. Which features actually matter?** | With **302,026** rows, chi-square and Mann-Whitney both return $p < 0.001$ for essentially everything, so significance alone carries no information and I report effect sizes next to the p-values. The honest summary: apart from `answer_count`, the associations are weak — Cramér's V below **0.11** for the categorical features and absolute Spearman correlations below **0.12** for the numeric ones. The features that survive on effect size and on business plausibility are code blocks, error text, body length, account tenure and posting time, and even those are modest individually. That is exactly why the hand-off to modelling is a set of structural and lagged features rather than one dominant variable, and why the model's lift over a no-skill ranking is modest. |
| **6** | **Your strongest correlate is answer count. Why is it not a feature?** | Because it is **leakage**. `answer_count` only exists once answers have arrived, which is after the moment we are asked to predict, and it has the largest effect size in the whole EDA (rank-biserial $-0.35$; Spearman **0.33** against the resolved target and **0.65** against the answered target). Using it would produce a beautiful validation score and a useless model in production. It is on the forbidden list in `docs/feature_spec.md` alongside `score`, `views`, `is_answered` and `closed_status`, and I raised it explicitly during the EDA so nobody later "improved" the model by adding it back. The same logic applies to tag volume: I recommend the **lagged** 4-week version (full weeks before posting), never the current one, so no future information enters the feature matrix. |
| **7** | **Walk me through how your EDA changed what the model actually builds.** | The `[M3-12]` section is a top-5 insight list where each insight names the feature and the owning task. Code blocks and error text produced `n_code_blocks`, `code_ratio` and `has_error_text`; account maturity produced `new_account` (under 7 days), continuous `account_age_days` and `missing_account_age`; timing produced `hour_posted`, `weekday_posted` and `month_posted`; the post-2022 tag shift produced **lagged** `lagged_tag_volume` and `lagged_tag_change` plus multi-hot tag encoding and `other_tags_count`; title semantics produced `title_tfidf_svd_*`, roughly 100 components, fitted inside the pipeline on training splits only. The 7-day new-account threshold is not arbitrary — the resolution rate plateaus immediately after seven days, so the bracket boundary follows the data. |
| **8** | **How do you read the time-to-answer distributions, and why did you transform them?** | Both distributions are heavily right-skewed, so I plot them on a $\ln(1 + \text{hours})$ scale; on a linear axis the bulk of first answers collapses into a single bar at zero. On the log scale the shape is informative: first answers pile up at near-zero hours, while hours-to-accepted has a wide right tail, meaning the community still answers fast and the *validation* step is what became slow. The per-tag split shows powerbi and android with broader, slower accept distributions than pandas, html, python and javascript, and the monthly weighted medians move together and become more volatile after the cutoff. Since the 7-day target truncates these distributions, they describe the same phenomenon from the latency side rather than duplicating the target analysis. |
| **9** | **(Another member's area) Explain Member 1's sampling weights and Member 4's leakage safeguards.** | Member 1 collects whole sampled days rather than individual questions, which avoids diurnal and weekday bias, and weights each sampled day by the ratio of days of that type in the month to days actually sampled, so weighted sums reproduce population volume (under 1% annualised error in the weighted monthly rates I plot, and within 0.01% of SEDE on Member 1's weekly population series). Member 4 turns that into a chronological split — train on 2019-2024, validate on Jan-Jun 2025, test on Jul 2025 onward — with time-series folds for cross-validation, so no future month informs an earlier prediction. Member 5 confirmed the test set was scored exactly once, which is why I can compare the EDA's pre- and post-2022 patterns against a model evaluated on the same period without tuning on it. |
